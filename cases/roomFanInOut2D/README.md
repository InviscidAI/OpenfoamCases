# Room Fan In vs Out — 2-D

Two runs of one OpenFOAM v2512 case that differ in a single setting: whether a fan in a
wall opening blows into a room or out of it. The pair backs a published clip; the point of
the case being here is that the clip can be checked against it.

- Domain: `4.00 x 3.00 m` plan, **two-dimensional** — one cell thick with `empty` front
  and back
- Mesh: `~26,175` fluid cells at `0.02 m` nominal spacing, generated by
  `scripts/generate_room_mesh.py`. Passes `checkMesh -allGeometry -allTopology`
- Solver: `pimpleFoam`, incompressible, isothermal, `nu = 1.5e-05`
- Turbulence: k-epsilon RAS; momentum `Gauss limitedLinearV 1`
- Openings: `fan` on the `x = 0` wall spanning `y 0.99..2.01`; `vent` on the `y = 3` wall
  spanning `x 2.90..3.92`

## The one setting that differs

```sh
$ python3 scripts/write_initial_fields.py --direction in  --into /tmp/fan-in
$ python3 scripts/write_initial_fields.py --direction out --into /tmp/fan-out
$ diff -r /tmp/fan-in /tmp/fan-out
diff -r /tmp/fan-in/p /tmp/fan-out/p
30c30
<         direction       in;
---
>         direction       out;
```

One line, in one file, out of five fields.

The direction is an **argument to `Allrun`**, not a second copy of the case. The
comparison is only worth something if nothing else differs, and one set of files makes
that true by construction instead of by inspection.

## Why two dimensions

Not a simplification of convenience. The clip is a plan view, and in 3-D a plan view could
not show this subject: seeding streamlines across the fan and integrating, only about **3%**
of the air that turned toward the vent passed within 0.15 m of the rendered plane. The rest
went over and under it — the flow is genuinely three-dimensional and no single plane
contains the story. Going 2-D makes the plan view *be* the solution domain instead of a
sample of it.

What that costs: 2-D removes floor and ceiling friction, which are the largest wall areas
in a room this shape. Expect a 2-D room to circulate more freely than the same room in 3-D,
and do not treat a 3-D version of this case as interchangeable with it.

## Mesh generation with cut-outs

`scripts/generate_room_mesh.py` splits the block grid at every coordinate where a feature
starts or ends, drops blocks whose centre lies inside a fitted solid, and turns the faces
newly exposed by those drops into the `furniture` patch.

Splitting at feature coordinates is what puts openings on exact cell boundaries, so `fan`
spans precisely `y 0.99..2.01` rather than the nearest cell edge to it. Everything this
case claims about its geometry is therefore true of the mesh, which is the point of
generating it rather than describing it.

## Boundary conditions worth looking at

| patch | `p` | `U` |
|---|---|---|
| `fan` | `fanPressure`, curve from `constant/fanCurve.dat` | `pressureInletOutletVelocity` |
| `vent` | `totalPressure`, `p0 = 0` | `pressureInletOutletVelocity` |
| `walls`, `furniture` | `zeroGradient` | `noSlip` |

Both of these are load-bearing and an earlier version of this study got both wrong:

- **A fixed-velocity fan is a pump with no stall.** It delivers its flow at any back
  pressure, so it can do unbounded work on the room. Replacing it with a pressure–flow
  curve cut the extraction case's peak speed from 21 m/s to 1.1.
- **A `fixedValue` static pressure on an opening that has inflow through it supplies
  kinetic energy for free.** Measured on the earlier 3-D version, that boundary was
  donating 91 of the 103 units of mechanical power the extraction room received — about
  124 W of air power where a domestic fan delivers 5–15. `totalPressure` pins total head
  to zero on inflow and removes it.

The boundary energy budget is the cheap way to catch both: sum
`(p + 0.5|U|^2)(U . n) dA` over the patches and a boundary that is doing net work on the
fluid shows up immediately.

## Furniture

Three fitted solids are removed from the mesh; their exposed faces are the `furniture`
patch with wall functions.

| item | wall | extent [m] |
|---|---|---|
| fitted wardrobe | y = 0 | x 0.40–1.40, y 0.00–0.60 |
| tall pantry/cabinet run | y = 3 | x 0.30–1.80, y 2.50–3.00 |
| built-in bookcase | x = 4 | x 3.70–4.00, y 0.20–0.80 |

**Only floor-to-ceiling furniture, and that is a constraint rather than a choice.** In a
2-D plan every obstacle is full height by construction — fair for a fitted wardrobe, wrong
for a bed, because air goes over a bed and a 2-D bed is a partition wall. The room is
therefore furnished as a fitted utility room. Any low furniture in the published clip is
drawn over the picture and is not in this case; the flow runs through it there, and stops
dead at these three.

Furnishing is not cosmetic here. It cut circulation by 4.7% supplying and 9.0%
extracting, and moved the headline difference from 19.7% to 14.2%.

## Results

Settled values, `t = 220..240` means from cold-start pilots:

| | supply (`in`) | extraction (`out`) |
|---|---|---|
| through-flow | 1.09510 m³/s | 1.09555 m³/s |
| domain-mean speed | 0.18834 m/s | 0.21509 m/s |

Through-flow is **equal** — 0.04% apart, which is not a difference. Direction does not
change how much air is exchanged, because the fan sits on its curve either way. What it
changes is mixing: extraction moves 14.2% more of the room.

## Running

```sh
./Allrun in      # or: ./Allrun out
```

Mesh, initial fields and patches are all generated, so the case runs from a clean clone.
`constant/polyMesh` is not tracked.

The published clip is the flow **developing from rest** over `t = 2..60 s`, 481 writes —
not a settled field. A settled 2-D room here is visually static: over the last 40 s of a
long run its maximum speed moves 0.0–0.5%. The establishing flow is the part with anything
to show, so the initial condition is a real modelling choice rather than a formality.

The end of development was chosen from pilots continued to `t = 240`: the last monitored
quantity to enter and stay within 0.5% of its `t = 220..240` mean was supply mean speed at
`t = 50.9 s`, and at `t = 60` the RMS velocity difference from `t = 240` is 0.0011 m/s
(supply) and 0.0003 (extraction). Start at 2 s rather than 0 to omit the uniform-`k`/
`epsilon` startup artefact.

The same room **empty** needed `t = 100` to reach the same criterion. Furniture shortens
settling by breaking up the slow room-scale recirculation, so a development window should
not be inherited across a geometry change.

## Limits

- **The fan curve is representative, not a measured product.** 25 Pa shutoff, 1.10 m³/s
  free delivery, parabolic between. The operating point lands near free delivery at
  0–2.3 Pa, so the curve is barely loaded and never reaches the 10–30 Pa duty region. A
  different curve could move the mixing result.
- **The fan opening is 1.02 m², which is large.** The curve fixes the volume, so what this
  understates is the jet's *momentum*: 1.1 m³/s through 1.02 m² is 1.1 m/s at the face,
  where a smaller opening passing the same air would be several times that. Jet momentum
  drives mixing, which is the one result here with a winner.
- **Two dimensions.** No flow over or under anything, no vertical motion, floor and
  ceiling friction absent. A 3-D version of this room is *not* interchangeable with it.
- **k-epsilon, no boundary-layer refinement.** Room-scale structure, not wall jets.
- **One geometry.** Where the second opening sits changes the result; an earlier version
  with the vent directly opposite the fan behaved as a duct, and both directions collapsed
  onto the same axial jet.
- **Speed is a proxy for the question asked.** "Which ventilates better" is about air
  exchange; age-of-air or a passive tracer would measure it rather than infer it.
